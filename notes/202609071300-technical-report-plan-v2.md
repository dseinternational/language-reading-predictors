## 1. The brief

The technical report (`docs/report/`, a Quarto book) must:

- **Report in full what was done and found**, in enough detail that a methods-literate reader could replicate the analysis. Technical detail may live in appendices, but nothing may be omitted.
- **Be accessible in parts to general audiences** who want more than a lay summary — families, teachers, teaching assistants, speech and language therapists, charity staff and funders who are willing to read carefully.
- **Put the work in a clear practical context** — what the Reading and Language Intervention (RLI) is, why predictors of progress matter, what a re-analysis of a 2012 trial can and cannot add.
- **Draw clear practical interpretations** of the findings, each tied to its evidence strength and causal status, and each paired with an explicit statement of what it does _not_ mean.

The deliverable of this note is a structure, a chapter-by-chapter contents proposal, a production plan and a list of author decisions. It writes no chapter.

## 4. Audiences and the accessibility design

The June decision to keep this a technical report stands. What changes is that the report carries a **deliberate accessible register in named places**, so that a general reader can follow a marked path through it without the document pretending to be two things at once. Three readers, three paths:

| Reader                                                                                           | What they need                                                                                                     | Their path through the report                                                                                                                                                                  |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **General reader** (family member, teacher, TA, therapist, funder) who wants more than a leaflet | The story, the size of the effects in test items, who benefited, what it means for practice, what it does not mean | Plain-language summary → Key findings at a glance → Chapter 1 (why this study) → the **"In brief"** box that opens every results chapter → Chapter 15 (what this means in practice) → Glossary |
| **Methods-literate researcher or practitioner**, fluent in p-values, newer to Bayesian output    | The design, the estimands, the numbers with uncertainty, the causal boundary, the diagnostics                      | Everything in the main text, with the reader's guide (Chapter 6) before the results                                                                                                            |
| **Replicator or auditor**                                                                        | Exact data, code, priors, commands, checksums, per-model diagnostics, decision history                             | Part II in full → Part III → Appendices B–F → the repository                                                                                                                                   |

Devices that make the first path work without a separate document:

- A **plain-language summary** of about two pages, written last, in the register of the sample in §12.
- A one-page **Key findings at a glance** spread: the per-outcome evidence table and the four-design agreement table, each with a plain sentence beneath.
- Every results chapter opens with an **"In brief"** box: three to six plain sentences stating the finding, how sure we are, and the boundary of the claim. The technical evidence follows. Every results chapter closes with **"What this does not show."**
- **Causal-status labels** on every results heading — `[CAUSAL]`, `[ROBUSTNESS]`, `[ASSOCIATION]`, `[DESCRIPTIVE]` — so the boundary is a property of the table of contents.
- Effects always stated **in test items** on the test's own scale, with the scale named ("+2.4 words of 79"), never logits alone.
- A **practical-interpretation chapter** whose every claim carries its evidence label and causal status and a "does not mean" clause (§7).
- The **glossary** and a **"How to read this report"** router in the front matter.
- Technical depth pushed to appendices and to the per-model reports, which are linked by model id.

### Part I — Background and aims

**Chapter 1. Language, reading and Down syndrome: why this study.** Purpose: the practical context for a general reader. Contents: the profile (language more affected than nonverbal ability; vocabulary a relative strength, grammar and speech weaker; verbal short-term memory weak); reading is achievable and often a relative strength; the phonological-awareness debate and its contemporary resolution; the Simple View and the Reading-is-Language model, with the DSE position that reading can support language and the Byrne 2002 caution; the intervention evidence (gains concentrate in taught skills, small samples, little on who responds); the RLI trial of 2012 in one page — design, sample, programme, the four primary outcomes that improved, the predictors reported then (younger, more sessions, better receptive language); why re-analyse (uncertainty quantified; direction separated from size; predictors estimated jointly across outcomes; the causal boundary made explicit; the data shared openly); the two cohorts and their roles. **Chapter 2. The questions, and what this design can answer.** Purpose: state the questions as estimands and set expectations. ### Part IV — Findings

**Chapter 8 [CAUSAL]. Did the intervention work, and on what?** In brief: after 20 weeks the children taught first knew about 3.5 more letter sounds of 32, read about 2.4 more words of 79, knew 1.5 and 1.4 more of the 24 taught words, and blended more items; broad standardised vocabulary showed no detectable change; four model designs agree. The evidence: the per-outcome table with the mandated sentence shape and evidence labels (letter sounds very strong; word reading, taught expressive vocabulary and blending strong; taught receptive and not-taught receptive vocabulary moderate; basic concepts, expressive grammar, receptive grammar, not-taught expressive vocabulary suggestive; nonword off-floor suggestive; spelling off-floor, expressive information and the two broad vocabulary tests inconclusive); the τ forest figure; the four-design agreement table and what agreement does and does not mean; direction versus size — P(benefit ≥ δ) 0.91 letter sounds, 0.90 word reading, 0.78 and 0.69 taught vocabulary, 0.49 blending, 0.24 and 0.19 broad vocabulary — with the δ-sensitivity sweep; the effect gradient from taught skills through word reading to untaught material to broad vocabulary, and why that is compatible with concentration on what was taught without proving absence elsewhere (intervals ±3–4 items); phoneme blending under both links (+1.0 versus +0.5 in `itt`; smaller under the floor in every family) and the defensible sentence ("blending improved") versus the indefensible one ("+1 item"); the floored outcomes as off-floor risk differences with mover counts and the 10/15/20 pp grid; generalisation — the joint taught-versus-not-taught contrasts (expressive +3.4 pp suggestive; receptive inconclusive) and the dependence-aware companions that change nothing; the block-2 exposure contrasts (no coherent effect); expressive grammar as the strongest lean among untaught outcomes, with the caution about a possible baseline imbalance on expressive information. Robustness: ability-, SES- and site-adjusted refits; the treatment-prior sweeps; the word-reading missing-data envelope (MAR +2.0; reference-based +1.7; model-free bounds −6.3 to +4.9) beside the letter-sound and taught-vocabulary bounds that cannot reverse; the DiD word-reading estimate as prior-informed; predicted-scores contrast panels. Later timepoints: the t3 arm gap as a randomised early-versus-delayed-start schedule contrast with no mechanistic reading; no evidence of wearing off or catch-up; dose as observational (+1.9 words and +1.6 letter sounds per interquartile step in sessions, moderate; attendance not randomised). What this does not show: effects for all 57 children; effects beyond 20 weeks of treated-versus-untreated comparison; which component of the programme did the work. Sources: findings 01, 02, 03, 04, 07, 09, 10; by-question Q1. **A** tables and figures; **H** prose.

**Chapter 9 [CAUSAL → ASSOCIATION]. How did word reading improve? Routes and mechanisms.** In brief: the models locate almost the whole word-reading gain on letter-sound knowledge and none on vocabulary, blending or nonword decoding; letter sounds have a decoding-specific excess association that survives ability adjustment; but most of the letter-sound-to-reading association separates children rather than tracking a child over time, the programme taught letter sounds and reading together, and the mediation effects are not identified — so this is the best-supported route, not a demonstrated channel. The evidence: the headline word-reading result restated with its envelope (`itt-010`, 53 children); the mediation table (letter sounds NIE +1.8 to +2.1 words of a +2.1 to +2.3 total; rivals near zero; grammar negative control +0.1; period-stacked companion allocates most to the direct path; nonword off-floor gain likewise allocated to letter sounds); the three non-identification obstacles (treatment-induced dose confounding, latent ability, contemporaneous timing) and the dose-confounding calibration (a plausible amount of confounding accounts for the indirect effect); the interventional companions as relabellings; the reverse-direction fits; the mechanism family — letter-sound slope on nonword decoding +1.03 versus word reading +0.25 per SD, identified contrast +0.81 [+0.49, +1.13]; negative controls not zero; ability adjustment leaves the code slopes untouched; the between/within split (+0.44 between, +0.03 within); the collapse of the sessions-to-reading curve once zero-attendance rows are excluded; moderation — every moderator gives a small negative product term, only blending and age survive re-expression in words, and the ceiling caution; the latent-change models (prior letter-sound level predicts later reading change, +0.6 to +0.8 SD per SD; prior change does not; letter sounds out-predict vocabulary per SD); the pooled-levels split (+1.61 between, +0.04 within); the survival timing result (children with more letter sounds leave the nonword floor sooner). The two-sided synthesis (pattern 2 of the September overview) written out. What this does not show: that teaching letter sounds alone, or more of them, would raise reading; any causal mediation; that decoding gates the letter-sound benefit (the data point the other way). Sources: findings 05, 06, 08, 14, 21, 11; by-question Q2, Q5. **A** tables, mechanism curves, mediation forest; **H** prose.

**Chapter 10 [ASSOCIATION]. Who made progress? Predictors of gain.** In brief: gains were only weakly predictable; the child's own starting score dominates; younger children gained more at the same starting point in every family and both cohorts; nonverbal ability tracked gains in oral language and letter sounds but not word reading or decoding; phonological memory tracked vocabulary and decoding gains; the hearing-risk flag leaned positive for reading gain and should be recorded, not explained; nothing survives a sceptical prior; background characteristics never predicted progress. The evidence: the gain-factors table for word reading (own baseline; age −1.0 words per SD, very strong; taught expressive vocabulary, letter sounds and nonword decoding moderate; the rest inconclusive; the randomised term as the only causal row); the age-not-time analysis (period intercepts carry the later-period decline; 99.9 % of remaining age variation is between children); the whole-study baseline predictors (`adj-065`: age −2.9 words per SD; hearing flag +2.2; behaviour −1.9); the horseshoe rankings (age first, then letter sounds; no predictor reaches P ≥ 0.75 of a non-negligible coefficient); the cross-outcome pattern table (age negative in every gain model; ability → oral-language and letter-sound gains, flat for word reading and decoding; phonological memory → vocabulary, decoding and off-floor outcomes; taught vocabulary at a period's start → later grammar, basic-concept and blending gains; speech production as the one exposure with a within-child reading signal); the moderation variants (lower starters show a larger on-intervention association in ten of eleven outcomes; model-dependent, partly post-crossover, never causal); the growth-curve results (ability predicts grammar growth, not reading growth; the historical cohort's negative verbal-reasoning slope to be explained by selection and ceilings); the survival family (hazard ratio 1.6 [0.85, 3.0] for leaving the nonword floor; covariates order more clearly than arm). The gradient-boosting corroboration in one page: gain models median R² 0.13, own baseline first in 19 of 22, age negative in 18 of 22, background variables never replicate; word-reading gain R² 0.08; the two layers agree that gain is barely predictable and add no predictor the Bayesian layer missed. What this does not show: levers; who will respond; a developmental law about age (a 5–10 age band, bounded tests, selection). Sources: findings 03, 20, 16, 17, 11; GB review §3.2, §4; by-question Q3. **A** tables; **H** prose.

**Chapter 11 [ASSOCIATION]. How the skills hang together: levels.** In brief: at every wave letter sounds and word reading are each other's strongest correlate (about +9 words per SD at timepoint 2); the vocabulary measures, grammar and basic concepts form one block; latent vocabulary, code and grammar domains correlate at 0.6 to 0.9 once measurement error is removed and about 95 % of each is stable child trait; levels are easy to predict and gains hard, and several apparent disagreements in the project are that distinction in disguise. The evidence: the concurrent per-timepoint table; the pooled-levels between/within split for each exposure and the no-wave-intercept comparator that manufactures a spurious within-child association; the latent domain correlations by wave; the horseshoe level rankings; the GB level models (median R² 0.61; three measurement blocks — language, speech-production/verbal-memory, reading — and the two bridges, letter sounds and expressive vocabulary; agreement with the Bayesian layer, rank correlation +0.54 for word-reading level); the historical cohort's verbal reasoning as the strongest correlate of reading. What this does not show: direction; anything beyond "which children are ahead". Sources: findings 13, 21, 15, 16; GB review §3.3; by-question Q4. **A**; **H**.

**Chapter 12 [ASSOCIATION]. Vocabulary, phonological memory, hearing and speech.** In brief: expressive vocabulary is a moderately supported correlate of reading on every view except the mutually adjusted gain model and predicts later reading change from a prior level; receptive vocabulary is weaker and appears where children read more; word repetition behaves like a code-adjacent skill; speech accuracy goes with reading level and with decoding, not with gain; the hearing-risk flag is associated with slightly larger reading gains, which is counter-intuitive, consistent across models, coarse, and not to be explained; no sign the intervention worked less well for children with hearing or speech difficulty, though the trial cannot resolve it. The evidence: the side-by-side vocabulary and repetition table; the speech and hearing tables; the arm-by-subgroup raw gains with their standard errors; the adequacy check on the hearing flag as an adjuster. What this does not show: that vocabulary helps reading (as opposed to faster-developing children having both); a hearing effect. Sources: by-question Q6, Q7; findings 06, 20, 21. **A**; **H**.

**Chapter 13 [DESCRIPTIVE]. The historical cohort: natural history and replication.** In brief: in a separate observational cohort assessed annually, children with Down syndrome gained about 21 words of 90 over five waves; stable levels of reading, vocabulary and digit recall correlate at +0.5 to +0.8 between children; within-child correlations are unresolved; the age and vocabulary–reading findings replicate under a common estimator across cohorts; six historical fits are withheld for want of confirmed test maxima. The evidence: the historical growth summaries by group; the historical joint correlations; the matched cross-cohort replication (age −0.18 versus −0.28 SD; vocabulary–reading r = 0.66 in both; verbal memory unresolved in both); the adjusted, concurrent and horseshoe ports. What this does not show: anything causal; comparability of magnitudes across instruments. Sources: findings 18, 19, 20; `notes/202608161600-cross-cohort-replication.md`; Byrne 2002. **A**; **H**.

## 8. Presentation rules

- **Result sentence shape**, everywhere a quantity is stated: items-scale median, 89 % equal-tailed interval, P(direction), then P(benefit ≥ δ) with δ named where a practical claim is made, then the evidence label — never a bare point or a bare label. Labels qualify a named claim, follow the probability, orient to the favoured direction, and never connote size.
- **Scale**: items on the test's own scale, with the maximum named; percentage points for the off-floor outcomes; logits only where the items translation does not exist, and then said so.
- **Causal language** only for the randomised timepoint-2 contrasts and the LCSM window-1 contrasts under their stated assumptions; "associated with, after adjusting for …" for everything else; "randomised schedule contrast, no mechanistic reading" for the timepoint-3 and -4 arm quantities; "model-based decomposition under stated assumptions" for mediation.
- **No hand-entered numbers**: every value through `_report_data.qmd` helpers reading the fit artefacts, gated on the release decision, degrading to a visible placeholder.
- **Blending** always as the pair, side by side.
- **Standing caveats** transcluded once from `_caveats-*.qmd`.
- **Figures**: the DAG; the CONSORT flow; the trajectory plot by arm and wave; the τ forest; predicted-scores contrast panels for the headline outcomes; the four-design agreement figure; the mediation forest; the letter-sound mechanism curve on the items scale with the interquartile worked example; the between/within figure; the gain-predictor forest; the concurrent heat-map by wave; the latent-domain correlation figure; the GB flagship beeswarms. All static PNG for print parity.
- **Print parity**: no folded content in the main text; depth to footnotes and appendices; HTML may add collapsible detail but must not carry content the PDF lacks.
- **House conventions**: British English; AI-authorship callouts at the top of every AI-drafted file, naming tool and model; paragraphs as single lines in source; DOIs on every citation, verified against the primary source before the citation enters the text; positive τ means the intervention arm did better; no p-values.
- **Register markers**: "In brief" and "What this does not show" boxes have fixed titles and fixed placement so the general reader can find them by scanning.

## 12. Sample of the accessible register: a draft plain-language summary

_Offered so the authors can judge the register before any chapter is written. Every number is a placeholder from the September notes._

> **What this report is about.** In 2012 a trial in mainstream schools in Yorkshire and Hampshire tested a daily reading and language programme for 57 children with Down syndrome aged five to ten. Trained teaching assistants delivered it one-to-one for 40 minutes a day. Half the children started at once; the other half waited 20 weeks and then started. That wait is what lets us compare taught with not-yet-taught children fairly, because chance alone decided who waited. This report re-analyses the trial's data, which are shared openly, with newer statistical methods. It asks two kinds of question: what the programme changed, and what predicted which children made most progress.
>
> **What the programme changed.** After 20 weeks the children who started first knew about 3.5 more letter sounds out of 32 than the children still waiting, read about 2.4 more words out of 79, and knew about one and a half more of the 24 words the programme had taught. We are very confident about the direction of these gains. The size is modest but, for reading and letter sounds, very probably worth having. Scores on broad vocabulary tests, which the programme also hoped to raise, did not detectably change in 20 weeks. That does not prove the programme cannot affect broad vocabulary; it means any effect was smaller than these tests can show in 54 children.
>
> **How the reading gain seems to have happened.** Everything in the data points to letter-sound knowledge: children who gained letter sounds gained words, and the reading gain is statistically carried almost entirely by the letter-sound gain rather than by vocabulary or blending. But the programme taught letter sounds and reading together, so we cannot say that teaching letter sounds alone would have the same effect.
>
> **Who made most progress.** Younger children gained more than older children who started at the same level. Children with better verbal short-term memory gained more vocabulary. Beyond that, progress was hard to predict, and nothing about a child's family background, hearing, behaviour or gender predicted it. Children with hearing difficulties or less clear speech did not benefit less.
>
> **What to be careful about.** This was 54 children in one trial, re-analysed many times over. Only the comparison of taught with not-yet-taught children at 20 weeks is a true cause-and-effect result; everything about predictors describes which children progressed, not what would change their progress. The tests of decoding and spelling were too hard for most children to show change at all. All findings are preliminary.
