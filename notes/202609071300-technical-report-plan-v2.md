## 1. The brief

The technical report (`docs/report/`, a Quarto book) must:

- Report all methods and findings needed to replicate the analysis; technical detail may sit in appendices.
- Offer an accessible route for families, teachers, teaching assistants, therapists, charity staff and funders who want more than a lay summary.
- Explain the Reading and Language Intervention (RLI), why predictors of progress matter, and what this reanalysis can add.
- Tie practical interpretations to their evidence strength, causal status and limits.

This note proposes the structure, chapter contents, production sequence and author decisions.

## 4. Audiences and the accessibility design

The report remains technical, with a marked reading route for each audience:

| Reader                                                                                           | What they need                                                                                                     | Their path through the report                                                                                                                                                                  |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **General reader** (family member, teacher, TA, therapist, funder) who wants more than a leaflet | The story, the size of the effects in test items, who benefited, what it means for practice, what it does not mean | Plain-language summary → Key findings at a glance → Chapter 1 (why this study) → the **"In brief"** box that opens every results chapter → Chapter 14 (what this means in practice) → Glossary |
| **Methods-literate researcher or practitioner**, fluent in p-values, newer to Bayesian output    | The design, the estimands, the numbers with uncertainty, the causal boundary, the diagnostics                      | Everything in the main text, with the reader's guide (Chapter 6) before the results                                                                                                            |
| **Replicator or auditor**                                                                        | Exact data, code, priors, commands, checksums, per-model diagnostics, decision history                             | Part II in full → Part III → Appendices B–F → the repository                                                                                                                                   |

The general-reader route uses:

- A **plain-language summary** of about two pages, written last, in the register of the sample in §12.
- A one-page **Key findings at a glance** spread: the per-outcome evidence table and the four-design agreement table, each with a plain sentence beneath.
- Every results chapter opens with an **"In brief"** box: three to six plain sentences stating the finding, how sure we are, and the boundary of the claim. The technical evidence follows. Every results chapter closes with **"What this does not show."**
- **Causal-status labels** on every results heading — `[CAUSAL]`, `[ROBUSTNESS]`, `[ASSOCIATION]`, `[DESCRIPTIVE]` — so the boundary is a property of the table of contents.
- Effects always stated **in test items** on the test's own scale, with the scale named ("+2.4 words of 79"), never logits alone.
- A **practical-interpretation chapter** whose every claim carries its evidence label and causal status and a "does not mean" clause (§7).
- The **glossary** and a **"How to read this report"** router in the front matter.
- Technical depth pushed to appendices and to the per-model reports, which are linked by model id.

## 8. Presentation rules

- **Result sentence shape**, everywhere a quantity is stated: items-scale median, 89 % equal-tailed interval, P(direction), then P(benefit ≥ δ) with δ named where a practical claim is made, then the evidence label — never a bare point or a bare label. Labels qualify a named claim, follow the probability, orient to the favoured direction, and never connote size.
- **Scale**: items on the test's own scale, with the maximum named; percentage points for the off-floor outcomes; logits only where the items translation does not exist, and then said so.
- **Causal language** only for the randomised timepoint-2 contrasts and the LCSM window-1 contrasts under their stated assumptions; "associated with, after adjusting for …" for everything else; "randomised schedule contrast, no mechanistic reading" for the timepoint-3 and -4 arm quantities; "model-based decomposition under stated assumptions" for mediation.
- **No hand-entered numbers**: every value through `_report_data.qmd` helpers reading the fit artefacts, gated on the release decision, degrading to a visible placeholder.
- **Blending** always as the pair, side by side.
- **Standing caveats** transcluded once from `_caveats-*.qmd`.
- **Figures**: the DAG; the CONSORT flow; the trajectory plot by arm and wave; the τ forest; predicted-scores contrast panels for the headline outcomes; the four-design agreement figure; the mediation forest; the letter-sound mechanism curve on the items scale with the interquartile worked example; the between/within figure; the gain-predictor forest; the concurrent heat-map by wave; the latent-domain correlation figure; the GB flagship beeswarms. All static PNG for print parity.
- **Print parity**: no folded content in the main text; depth to footnotes and appendices; HTML may add collapsible detail but must not carry content the PDF lacks.
- **House conventions**: British English; AI-authorship callouts at the top of every AI-drafted file, naming tool and model; paragraphs as single lines in source; DOIs on every citation, verified against the primary source before the citation enters the text; positive τ means the intervention arm did better; no p-values.
- **Register markers**: "In brief" and "What this does not show" boxes have fixed titles and fixed placement so the general reader can find them by scanning.

## 12. Sample of the accessible register: a draft plain-language summary

_Offered so the authors can judge the register before any chapter is written. Every number is a placeholder from the September notes._

> **What this report is about.** In 2012 a trial in mainstream schools in Yorkshire and Hampshire tested a daily reading and language programme for 57 children with Down syndrome aged five to ten. Trained teaching assistants delivered it one-to-one for 40 minutes a day. Half the children started at once; the other half waited 20 weeks and then started. That wait is what lets us compare taught with not-yet-taught children fairly, because chance alone decided who waited. This report re-analyses the trial's data, which are shared openly, with newer statistical methods. It asks two kinds of question: what the programme changed, and what predicted which children made most progress.
>
> **What the programme changed.** After 20 weeks the children who started first knew about 3.5 more letter sounds out of 32 than the children still waiting, read about 2.4 more words out of 79, and knew about one and a half more of the 24 words the programme had taught. We are very confident about the direction of these gains. The size is modest but, for reading and letter sounds, very probably worth having. Scores on broad vocabulary tests, which the programme also hoped to raise, did not detectably change in 20 weeks. That does not prove the programme cannot affect broad vocabulary; it means any effect was smaller than these tests can show in 54 children.
>
> **How the reading gain seems to have happened.** Everything in the data points to letter-sound knowledge: children who gained letter sounds gained words, and the reading gain is statistically carried almost entirely by the letter-sound gain rather than by vocabulary or blending. But the programme taught letter sounds and reading together, so we cannot say that teaching letter sounds alone would have the same effect.
>
> **Who made most progress.** Younger children gained more than older children who started at the same level. Children with better verbal short-term memory gained more vocabulary. Beyond that, progress was hard to predict, and nothing about a child's family background, hearing, behaviour or gender predicted it. Children with hearing difficulties or less clear speech did not benefit less.
>
> **What to be careful about.** This was 54 children in one trial, re-analysed many times over. Only the comparison of taught with not-yet-taught children at 20 weeks is a true cause-and-effect result; everything about predictors describes which children progressed, not what would change their progress. The tests of decoding and spelling were too hard for most children to show change at all. All findings are preliminary.
