# Findings overview, 2026-09-01: what the statistical models found, across all families

**This is the summary note for the 2026-09-01 findings series.** Twenty-one companion notes (`findings-01` to `findings-21`, same date) each report one model family — every model's result, the family's pattern, and what the family cannot support. This note does three things: it records the state of the evidence base the series is read from, it summarises each family in a paragraph, and it draws out the patterns that run across families and what they suggest about the predictors of language and reading development in this cohort. All results are preliminary and exploratory. This is work in progress, not a published trial report.

## The families in one paragraph each

**`itt` (31 models) — did the intervention work?** The headline trial estimates over the randomised timepoint-1-to-2 window. Letter-sound knowledge +3.5 items of 32 (89% +1.7 to +5.3, very strong), word reading +2.4 of 79 (+0.7 to +4.1, strong), taught expressive and receptive vocabulary +1.5 and +1.4 of 24 (strong and moderate), phoneme blending +1.0 of 10 under the ordinary link and +0.5 under the guessing floor, not-taught receptive vocabulary +0.6 (moderate). Basic concepts, expressive grammar, receptive grammar and not-taught expressive vocabulary lean positive at suggestive evidence; the nonword off-floor contrast is suggestive and the spelling one inconclusive, both in the floored subgroups; broad standardised vocabulary and expressive information sit at zero. Robust to ability, SES and site adjustment. The word-reading result survives every reasonable missingness assumption at about +2 items but its model-free extreme-case bound spans −6.3 to +4.9; the letter-sound and taught-vocabulary bounds cannot reverse.

**`did` (22) — the crossover design.** Randomised t2 contrasts that agree with `itt` (letter sounds +3.5, word reading +2.2, taught expressive +1.5, blending +0.9 or +0.6 under the floor, expressive grammar +1.6 at moderate evidence). The word-reading contrast is prior-informed (its magnitude moves from 1.3 to 2.5 items across the prior sweep while the direction holds) and is sensitive to how a realised baseline imbalance is allocated (+1.6 under the wider allocation priors). No pre-treatment gap is resolved; the post-crossover gaps mostly narrow, which describes how two randomised schedules diverged and identifies no mechanism. The dose companions are observational.

**`gain_factors` (33) — post-score given pre-score, stacked over periods.** The period-1-standardised contrast agrees again (letter sounds +3.3, word reading +2.6, blending +0.8, basic concepts +1.0, taught vocabulary +1.0 to +1.2). Receptive vocabulary leans negative (−2.0 items, suggestive) and the new period-1-only refits show that lean is carried by the stacked post-crossover rows. The family is also the project's main table of gain predictors: the own starting score dominates everywhere, age is negative for the reading, grammar and taught-vocabulary gains, non-verbal ability tracks gains in every oral-language outcome and in letter sounds but not in word reading or nonword decoding, phonological memory tracks vocabulary gains and both off-floor outcomes, and taught vocabulary at a period's start tracks later grammar, basic-concept and blending gains. The moderation variants find lower starters showing a larger on-intervention association in ten of eleven outcomes, model-dependent and not causal.

**`level_factors` (23) — scores at each timepoint.** The t2 change in the adjusted arm gap agrees with the suite (letter sounds +2.9, word reading +2.3, taught expressive +1.4) and the eleven two-wave comparators sit within a few tenths of an item of the four-wave estimates, so the longitudinal working model costs little. The immediate arm started slightly lower on the vocabulary measures, which is why its t2 vocabulary levels look negative while the randomised change is zero. One striking schedule contrast: by timepoint 4 the immediate arm's receptive-vocabulary standing had improved relative to the waiting arm's (very strong), reported without a mechanism.

**`mediation` and `mediation_multi` (20) — through what route?** In every fitted decomposition almost the whole word-reading gain is allocated to letter-sound knowledge (indirect +2.1 words, 89% +0.9 to +3.7; total +2.3) and essentially none to expressive vocabulary, blending or nonword decoding as rivals, with the taught-vocabulary single-mediator fits allocating little (+0.3 and +0.5 words); the grammar negative control allocates +0.1. The nonword off-floor gain is likewise allocated to letter sounds (+10 of +11 percentage points). But the natural effects are not identified (dose is treatment-induced, latent ability unmeasured, mediator and outcome contemporaneous), the dose-confounding calibration says session confounding could plausibly account for the indirect effect, the period-stacked companion allocates most of the effect to the direct path, and the reverse-direction fits show the machinery will route a later letter-sound gain through earlier reading at a smaller but resolved size.

**`mechanism` (46) — which skills track which.** Letter-sound knowledge carries a broad, low-level association with every skill measured, including the oral-language negative controls (0.10–0.12 logit per SD), plus a large excess for nonword decoding (1.03) over word reading (0.25); the identified nonword-minus-word contrast is +0.81 (89% +0.49 to +1.13). Partialling out measured ability leaves the written-code slopes untouched and most of the non-specific component standing, while ability itself predicts the oral-language outcomes and not the code ones. The between/within split of the letter-sound-to-word-reading slope puts almost all of it between children (+0.44 per SD) and the within-child part at inconclusive (+0.03). No curve has a qualified knee; the sessions-to-reading signal disappeared once zero-attendance rows were excluded; every moderator of the letter-sound slope returns a similar small negative product term, of which only blending and age survive re-expression in words. Phonological memory and speech production both track nonword decoding.

**`joint` (7) — all outcomes in one graph.** Reproduces the single-outcome contrasts; the taught-minus-not-taught contrasts do not establish limited transfer (expressive +3.4 percentage points, suggestive; receptive −0.3, inconclusive, with the not-taught receptive marginal clearing zero on its own), and modelling within-child residual dependence changes nothing material. The new-child predictive target is validated for the six two-outcome fits.

**`joint_mechanism` (2) — is the letter-sound route specific to decoding?** The transition design says yes on its own terms (Δ = +0.81); the levels design finds no stable sign across waves, which is what a shared reading-development component exaggerated by the nonword floor would produce. Both are operational contrasts between two test-score associations of tests with very different item counts and floors. Both withhold the importance-sampling new-child estimate; the K-fold route gives `jm-001` a complete estimate and `jm-002` an incomplete one.

**`dose_response` (6).** Among children on the intervention, an interquartile step in attended sessions goes with +1.9 words (89% +0.0 to +4.0, moderate) and +1.6 letter sounds (+0.1 to +2.9, moderate), and with no blending difference under either link. Attendance is not randomised; no dose can be recommended.

**`block_exposure` (5).** No coherent or well-determined block-2 teaching effect: the two taught outcomes point in opposite directions at suggestive evidence, the not-taught outcomes are inconclusive.

**`survival` (2).** Among children at the floor at baseline, the intervention arm's first-interval hazard of coming off the nonword floor is 1.6 times the waiting arm's (89% 0.85 to 3.0, suggestive) and 1.1 for phonetic spelling (inconclusive); baseline word reading and letter sounds predict an earlier exit more clearly than the arm does.

**`aligned` (10).** Onset-aligned 40-week cohort contrasts: letter sounds +2.2 (moderate) and word reading +2.1 (suggestive) in the immediate cohort's favour, but grammar −1.4 (moderate) and expressive vocabulary −3.1 (suggestive) against it — the age-at-onset and timing confound the design cannot remove, and a reason to read none of these as effects.

**`concurrent` (14).** Letter sounds and word reading are each other's strongest same-wave correlate at every wave (+9.0 words per SD of letter sounds at timepoint 2, with the estimate ranging from +7.2 to +11.0 across adjustment sets); the vocabulary measures cluster with each other and with grammar; word reading is the strongest correlate of receptive vocabulary at wave 3; in the historical cohort verbal reasoning is the strongest correlate of word reading at three of four waves. No direction, by construction.

**`lcsm` (5).** Prior letter-sound level predicts later word-reading change (+0.25 logits, 89% +0.14 to +0.38; +0.6 to +0.8 SD per SD), prior letter-sound _change_ does not, and per SD letter sounds out-predict vocabulary in the change-conditioned model; prior taught receptive vocabulary predicts later taught expressive change (+0.42), and prior word reading predicts later taught-vocabulary change a little; age predicts slower reading change. Temporal precedence, not causation.

**`corr_factor` and `long_corr_factor` (6).** Latent vocabulary, code and grammar domains correlate at +0.6 to +0.9 with measurement error removed, stably across all four waves, and about 95% of each domain's latent variance is stable child trait rather than wave-specific state. The structural code slope on reading gain is specification- and prior-sensitive.

**`horseshoe` (7).** For levels, letter sounds and word reading top each other's ranking with expressive vocabulary a close second; for gains, nothing survives the shrinkage in either cohort. The rankings broadly corroborate the gradient-boosting layer (top-3 overlaps of two in three for three of four pairings).

**`growth` (4).** Non-verbal ability predicts faster receptive-grammar growth (+0.11 logits per SD) but not reading growth; older-and-more-able children progress faster on letter sounds than the main effects imply; in the historical cohort higher verbal reasoning went with slower word-reading growth (−0.13 per SD, moderate, weakening to suggestive under the influence refit), a result to be explained by selection and ceilings before anything developmental.

**`historical_growth` (9) and `historical_joint` (3).** Children with Down syndrome in the historical cohort gained +21 words of 90 over five waves, with 15 of their 28 interval quantities clearing zero against 19 of 21 for average readers; stable levels of reading, vocabulary and digit recall correlate at +0.54 to +0.77 between children across the two designs, while within-child correlations are unresolved under either prior. Descriptive natural history in different children from the trial.

**`adjusted` (7).** Age is the strongest adjusted predictor of word-reading progress in both cohorts and negative in both (−2.9 words per SD in the trial, −2.7 items in the historical pooled model); the trial's hearing-risk flag and behaviour rating are also resolved (+2.2 and −1.9 words per SD), and letter sounds keep half their unadjusted association after mutual adjustment.

**`pooled_levels` (7).** Pooled over four waves, the letter-sound-to-word-reading association is almost entirely between children (+1.61 logits, about 17 words per SD) with a within-child part of +0.04; nonword decoding keeps a within-child part (+0.33, moderate); the vocabulary and phonological-memory exposures repeat the between-child pattern; speech production is the one exposure whose within-child association (+0.30, very strong) is a substantial fraction of its between-child one. Without wave intercepts, shared maturation manufactures a spurious within-child letter-sound association.

## Patterns across families

### 1. Four designs, one randomised answer

The intervention's effect on the skills it taught is the most robust result in the project, and it is robust in a specific sense: four model-based specifications of the same randomised window, with different adjustment, repeated-measures and likelihood assumptions, land in the same place.

These are not four independent experiments — they share children and window — but agreement across their different assumptions is the robustness check the design allows, and it holds. The latent-change window-1 contrasts in `lcsm` and the on-intervention indicator in `dose_response` point the same way. The effect gradient runs from the directly taught skills (letter sounds, taught words, blending) through word reading to material that was not taught (not-taught vocabulary, basic concepts, expressive grammar, all leaning positive at suggestive-to-moderate evidence) to the broad standardised vocabulary tests at zero. That gradient is compatible with an effect concentrated on what was taught and does not prove absence elsewhere: the broad vocabulary intervals are two and a half to five items wide in each direction.

Three things qualify the randomised answer without undermining it. The word-reading estimate rests on the analysed 53 children and its model-free missing-data bound can reverse; the letter-sound and taught-vocabulary bounds cannot. The DiD word-reading magnitude is prior-informed. And phoneme blending's size depends on whether guessing is modelled — every family's estimate is smaller under the floor, by half in `itt` and `gain_factors` and by a third or less elsewhere — so "blending improved" is the defensible sentence and "+1 item" is not.

### 2. Letter-sound knowledge is where the reading story concentrates — and where the shared-cause problem is sharpest

Every route-oriented family points at letter-sound knowledge. The mediation decompositions allocate the whole word-reading gain and the whole nonword off-floor gain to it. The mechanism family finds its association with nonword decoding nine times its association with the oral-language negative controls, and the identified decoding contrast passes clearly. The latent-change models find prior letter-sound level predicting later reading change and out-predicting vocabulary per SD. The concurrent and horseshoe families find letter sounds and word reading as each other's strongest correlate at every wave.

And every family that can separate _which children_ from _within a child_ finds the association living between children. The pooled-levels split puts the letter-sound-to-word-reading association at +1.61 logits between children and +0.04 within; the mechanism family's own split gives +0.44 and +0.03; the historical joint model resolves between-child correlations and no within-child ones; the longitudinal factor model attributes 95% of each domain's latent variance to stable trait. The negative controls are not zero, measured ability explains some of the non-specific component but leaves most of it standing, the latent domains correlate at 0.6 to 0.9, and the mediation machinery will route a later letter-sound gain through earlier reading as readily as the reverse.

The honest synthesis is therefore two-sided. There is a well-identified _excess_ association between letter sounds and decoding specifically — the pattern a decoding route predicts and a pure general-ability account does not, reproduced on a transition design, a levels split (for nonword decoding), a latent-change design and a g-formula. And there is a large shared component across every skill here that no adjustment set in the project can block, which is compatible with general ability, shared teaching, maturation or method variance, and which is where most of the letter-sound-to-word-reading association sits. What the randomised evidence adds is that teaching letter sounds and taught words raised word reading; what the associational evidence cannot add is that raising letter-sound knowledge by other means, or by more, would do the same.

### 3. Predictors of gain: a consistent, small set

Reading the families that ask who progressed — `gain_factors`, `adjusted`, `lcsm`, `growth`, `mechanism`, `pooled_levels`, `horseshoe` — the same handful of covariates recur, and they recur with the same sign.

- **Age is negative, everywhere it is fitted, for gains conditional on the starting score.** In the trial: −1.0 words per SD in `gain_factors`, −2.9 words in `adjusted`, −0.09 to −0.15 logits per SD of later reading change in `lcsm`, negative age couplings in every `mechanism` reading model, negative age main effects on the growth rate for all five measures in `growth`, resolved for four of them. In the historical cohort: −2.7 items in the pooled transitions model and −1.1 for digit recall. Older children in this cohort gained fewer items on bounded tests, conditional on where they started. Bounded-test headroom, item-scale deceleration and cohort composition are the candidate explanations; none is a claim about ageing.
- **Non-verbal ability tracks oral-language gains, and letter-sound gain, but not word reading or decoding.** It is a resolved positive associate of gains in receptive and expressive vocabulary, grammar, basic concepts, taught vocabulary and letter sounds, and flat for word reading and nonword decoding, in `gain_factors`; conditional on baseline it predicts the oral-language outcomes and not the written-code ones in the `mechanism` ability panel; it predicts grammar growth and not reading growth in `growth`. The split is consistent across three families that adjust differently.
- **Phonological memory (word and nonword repetition) tracks vocabulary and decoding.** Resolved positive associations with receptive-vocabulary and taught-vocabulary gains and with both off-floor outcomes (`gain_factors`), with word reading and nonword decoding conditional on baseline (`mechanism`), with taught-vocabulary change (`lcsm-081`), and between children with word reading (`pooled_levels`, +7 words per SD).
- **Taught vocabulary at the start of a period tracks later grammar, basic-concept and blending gains** (`gain_factors`), later taught-expressive change (`lcsm`, +0.42) and, conditional on the reading baseline, word reading (`mechanism`, +2.0 to +2.2 words across the interquartile range).
- **Speech production is the one exposure with a within-child signal for reading** (`pooled_levels`, +0.30 logits, very strong) and it tracks nonword decoding (`mechanism`), while in the latent-change models it is negatively coupled to taught-vocabulary change. Mixed, and worth a design that could test it.
- **The hearing-risk flag leans positive for reading gain** in the trial (`adjusted` +2.2 words per SD; the `corr_factor` structural leg +0.21 logits; weak positive leans on vocabulary gains in `gain_factors` and on reading, blending and taught-vocabulary change in `lcsm`). The composite is coarse, has nine unknowns, is not randomised and may proxy something else; the recurrence across families makes it worth recording, not worth explaining.
- **Nothing predicts gain strongly enough to survive a sceptical prior.** The horseshoe rankings of word-reading and letter-sound gain in the trial, and of vocabulary and grammar gain in the historical cohort, put their top predictor below the weakest positive rung. That is the right check on the list above: these are consistent, modest, adjusted associations in a cohort of 54 children, not a profile of who responds.

### 4. Levels and gains are different questions, and the data answer them differently

Levels are easy and gains are hard. The level models (`concurrent`, `horseshoe` level rankings, `pooled_levels` between-child terms, `corr_factor`) find large, stable, mutually reinforcing associations among all the skills; the gain models find small increments, strong regression to the starting score, and few resolved predictors. Several apparent disagreements in the project are this distinction in disguise: the joint-mechanism levels and transition designs, the `level_factors` vocabulary levels against its randomised change, the horseshoe level and gain rankings, and the pooled-levels comparator without wave intercepts, whose spurious within-child association is manufactured entirely by shared maturation.

### 5. What the floors and the thin measures cost

Nonword reading (6 items, 40–72% floored by wave) and phonetic spelling (heavily floored) are the outcomes the intervention is most plausibly aimed at through decoding, and they are the ones the data can say least about: the off-floor contrasts are suggestive at best in every family, the survival hazard ratio is 1.6 with an interval from 0.85 to 3.0, and the mediation nulls through nonword decoding are partly mechanical because the intervention barely moved most children off the floor. Phoneme blending's three-choice format shrinks its estimated effect under the guessing floor in every family, by half in `itt` and `gain_factors`. These are measurement limits the design carried in, not findings of no effect.

## What the findings suggest about predictors of language and reading development

With every caution above in force, the suite supports the following reading for this cohort of children with Down syndrome.

1. **Direct teaching of letter sounds and words produced measurable gains in those skills and in word reading within twenty weeks, and the gain generalised weakly if at all to broad vocabulary.** This is the one causal result, and it is reproduced across four specifications.
2. **Letter-sound knowledge is the strongest single correlate and the most plausible route to word reading and decoding in these data**, with a decoding-specific excess that survives ability adjustment; but most of its association with word reading separates children rather than tracking a child over time, so the evidence for a within-child mechanism rests on nonword decoding and on the randomised effect rather than on the associational slopes.
3. **Oral-language skills and general ability move together as a broadly correlated system** — latent vocabulary, code and grammar domains at 0.6 to 0.9, verbal reasoning as the strongest correlate of reading in the historical cohort — and non-verbal ability predicts progress in the oral-language domain and in letter-sound knowledge, not in word reading or decoding.
4. **Phonological memory and speech production are the language-side predictors that reach into the code domain**: memory into vocabulary gains and decoding, speech production into decoding and, uniquely, into within-child reading movement.
5. **Age is the most consistent negative predictor of item-scale gain**, conditional on the starting score, in both cohorts — an argument for reading the trial's effects as effects on young children with room to grow on bounded tests, and for caution in extrapolating them to older children.
6. **The data do not identify who will gain most.** Lower starters show a larger on-intervention association in the moderation variants, but that pattern is model-dependent, partly post-crossover and vulnerable to regression to the mean, and the sceptical-prior rankings flatten every gain predictor.

Points 2 to 6 are adjusted associations from a small cohort. They describe which children progressed, and they are consistent enough across families to be worth stating; they do not identify levers, and the project's causal diagram says its adjustment sets cannot make them do so.

## Reading the series

Each family note states its data, what the model is for, how to read its numbers, what it found, what changed since August, what it cannot support, and its model inventory with release status. Numbers are read from each fit's `key_findings.json` and family result tables under `output/statistical_models/models/<id>-reporting/` and from `output/statistical_models/comparison/`; every fit's convergence and release status was re-evaluated from the stored artefacts on 2026-09-01 rather than taken from the batch record. Posterior medians with 89% equal-tailed credible ranges and the posterior probability of the favoured direction are the reporting standard; the evidence labels are the project's fixed ladder (suggestive from 0.75, moderate from 0.91, strong from 0.97, very strong from 0.99), oriented to the favoured direction.
