# Findings: the `lcsm` family — do changes in one skill precede changes in another?

**Read `findings-00-overview` first.** This note covers the 5 latent change-score models. The cross-process couplings are associations, not causal paths. Four models also fit separate arm-by-window change intercepts: only their immediate-minus-wait-list contrast in the first, randomised window has a causal reading under the stated analysis-set assumptions; later-window arm contrasts do not.

## The data

**RLI trial only**, using **all four timepoints**: 54 children contributing 216 rows (four waves each). Data are stacked, not collapsed — the model needs the full sequence per child, because it is built around the transitions between consecutive waves.

## What the model is for

The `concurrent` family measures everything simultaneously and therefore cannot say what precedes what. This family adds **temporal ordering**: does a child's level of one skill at one wave predict how much _another_ skill **changes** by the next wave?

The model works in changes rather than levels. For each child and each interval it forms a latent change score — how much the underlying skill moved — and asks whether that change is predicted by a _prior_ level (a "g" coupling) or by a _prior change_ (an "h" coupling). "Latent" here means the model separates the underlying skill from measurement noise, using the Beta-Binomial structure to describe how observed test scores arise from it.

The cross-process couplings are reported on the latent log-odds scale per **unit** of the source's prior latent log-odds (or prior latent change), not per standard deviation — only the age term is standardised, and only the dominance contrast in `dominance_summary.csv` is put on a per-SD footing. Couplings are held constant across arms and transitions.

## What was found

| Coupling                                                                   | Estimate (logit units) | 89% range      |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------- | -------------- |
| Prior taught receptive vocabulary → change in taught expressive vocabulary | **+0.42**              | +0.25 to +0.62 |
| Prior letter sounds → change in word reading (`lcsm-082`)                  | **+0.24**              | +0.14 to +0.36 |
| Prior letter-sound _change_ → change in word reading (`lcsm-091`)          | +0.05                  | −0.17 to +0.26 |
| Age → change in reading                                                    | **−0.15**              | −0.21 to −0.09 |

**Prior letter-sound level predicts later word-reading change, but prior letter-sound change does not.** The level-to-change coupling is +0.24 [+0.14, +0.36] in `lcsm-082`, and the corresponding `g_L` term in `lcsm-091` is +0.22 [+0.12, +0.33]. The actual change-to-change headline in `lcsm-091`, `h_L`, is only +0.05 [−0.17, +0.26], P(>0) = 0.640: inconclusive. The results support temporal ordering of prior **level** and later change within the model, not the stronger claim that one interval's letter-sound gain predicts the next interval's reading gain.

**Higher prior taught receptive vocabulary is associated with greater subsequent change in taught expressive vocabulary** (+0.42 in `lcsm-081`, and +0.45 in its `lcsm-181` variant — the largest couplings in the family). This is an across-child prior-level-to-later-change association. It does not establish that individual children first recognise and then produce the same words, nor does this fit independently validate that developmental sequence.

**Age predicts _slower_ reading change** (−0.15). Read carefully: this does not mean children get worse. Within this sample and window, older children gained less than younger children, conditional on everything else in the model. With 54 children across a narrow age band, and age correlated with starting level, this is a fragile quantity and should not be built on.

A comparator model (`lcsm-181`) that removes the reverse coupling gives +0.45 for the vocabulary relationship against +0.42 with it, so the vocabulary result does not depend on modelling the reverse path.

## The separate randomised-window contrasts

The coupling coefficients above should not be confused with the models' arm-by-window change intercepts. In `lcsm-082`, the immediate-minus-wait-list contrast in latent change during the randomised first window is +0.404 for word reading (89% +0.093 to +0.726), +0.487 for phoneme blending (+0.066 to +0.921) and +0.685 for letter sounds (+0.310 to +1.058). These are on the latent log-odds change scale. Comparable window-1 contrasts appear in `lcsm-081`, `091` and `181`.

Those first-window assigned-arm contrasts inherit randomisation, subject to available-case and model assumptions. They are alternative model-based versions of the same trial window rather than independent experiments. The analogous arm contrasts in later windows occur after crossover and are associations.

## How much weight temporal precedence carries

Less than it appears. Temporal ordering rules out the simplest reverse-causation story, but it does not establish causation. A stable third factor — general ability, home literacy environment, schooling — that affects both letter-sound level and later reading growth could produce the same level-to-change pattern.

The `mechanism` family's negative-control result, where letter sounds also tracked oral-language outcomes, shows that part of the letter-sound association is not specific to the proposed route — though it sits alongside a much larger nonword-specific excess in those same models. A stable common factor is one plausible explanation of the non-specific part, but the negative controls do not uniquely identify it. The same residual-confounding concern applies here.

There is also a statistical caution specific to change-score models: they are demanding of data. Four waves and 54 children is modest, and separating latent change from measurement noise consumes information rapidly. The intervals above reflect that.

## What these models cannot tell you

**Temporal precedence is not causation.** A common cause acting earlier produces the same pattern.

**Couplings are held constant across arms and transitions.** If the relationship differed during intervention versus after crossover, this model would average over that difference rather than reveal it.

**The cross-process couplings are not treatment effects.** They are longitudinal associations pooled across arms, not pure within-child effects: with four waves and 54 children, stable between-child differences cannot be cleanly separated from within-child change. The separate first-window assigned-arm change contrasts described above are the only randomisation-anchored quantities in this family.

**The age coefficient is not a claim about ageing.** It is a within-sample association over a narrow band.
