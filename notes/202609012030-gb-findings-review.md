## 2. How to read the numbers

- **Pooled R²** is the out-of-fold R² against each fold's training mean. Because the hyperparameters were tuned on the same grouped folds, it is a **post-selection internal cross-validation** number (`cv_post_selection: true`), not an independent generalisation estimate. Mean absolute error (MAE) is in the outcome's own units and is quoted beside the outcome's standard deviation so the scale is visible.
- **Permutation importance** is the increase in held-out RMSE when a predictor is permuted between children. The `z` column is the permutation mean divided by its spread over the 50 repeats: it says how well the permutation estimate itself is resolved, not whether the association would replicate in new children. The subject-bootstrap **top-5 appearance rate** speaks to that second question. In this note a predictor is called **replicated** when both hold (z ≥ 2 and top-5 in at least half the bootstraps) and **borderline** when only one does. With roughly 33 predictors in each of 50 models, some z ≥ 2 hits are expected by chance, which is why the cross-model tables in section 3 count how often a predictor replicates rather than whether it ever does.
- **Direction** comes from the Spearman correlation between a predictor and its SHAP values (`feature_shap_spearman`) and the shape flag (`monotonic`, `noisy`, `non_monotonic`), checked against the beeswarm where it matters. A `+` means higher values of the predictor pushed the prediction up.
- **Clusters** group distance-correlated predictors; importance is shared within a cluster, so a cluster is the stable unit and the within-cluster order is not. **Same-skill siblings** (`same_skill_of_outcome`) are curated concurrent restatements of a level outcome (for example the Block-1 receptive total for receptive vocabulary, or the DEAP components for a DEAP composite); they are reported but discounted. Gain models flag no sibling by design: the own baseline is the regression-to-the-mean anchor, not contamination.
- **Language.** A ranked predictor "predicts" or "is associated with" an outcome in these data. Nothing here is a lever.

### 3.2 Gain models: the own baseline, then not much

In 19 of the 22 gain models the outcome's own baseline is the top permutation predictor, its SHAP direction is negative, and it carries between 42 % and 89 % of the model's positive permutation importance (z between 3.3 and 10.2). Higher starting scores predict smaller gains. That is regression to the mean on bounded, noisy scales, sharpened by ceilings (letter sounds, blending, vowel articulation) and floors (spelling, nonword reading, word reading), and it is not a developmental finding. The three exceptions are instructive rather than contradictory:

- **Word reading gain** (`lrp-rli-gbg-012`) is the one model that does not use its own baseline: permuting it slightly _improves_ held-out accuracy (z −2.3) and its SHAP contribution is at most 0.02 words. The tuned model (minimum 39 rows per leaf, depth 4) keeps a single split near eight words and otherwise ignores the baseline. The likely reason is that the baseline-to-gain relation is hump-shaped for word reading: 39 % of children were at zero at wave 1 and gained little, children in the middle gained most, and the highest scorers regressed, which a shallow monotone-leaning tree cannot exploit and which the Bayesian gain-factor model handles through its own-baseline coupling instead.
- **Phonetic-spelling gain** (`lrp-rli-gbg-011`) keeps its baseline first but with a non-monotonic shape (Spearman −0.15): the SHAP scatter is an inverted U, near zero at the floor, strongly positive (+4 to +10 points) for baselines of roughly 8–30, and falling to −8 near the top of the scale. Children who had just come off the floor gained most; children still at zero (78 % at wave 1) mostly stayed there.
- **Total repetition** and **average articulation** gains (`lrp-rli-gbg-019`, `-022`) are led by a component score rather than the composite because both components sit in the predictor set; they are component analyses in disguise and should be read next to `-017`/`-018` and `-016`/`-020`/`-021` rather than on their own.

Beyond the own baseline, the gain models are sparse. Applying the replicated criterion (z ≥ 2 and top-5 in at least half the bootstraps) and excluding the own baseline leaves 13 predictors that replicate anywhere, none in more than four of the 22 models, and eight of the 22 models replicate nothing at all. Two of the `deappfi` hits (the two DEAP outcomes) and the negative `erbnw` hit (the ERB total) are within-instrument, so the cross-domain list is shorter still. Read as a whole it sorts into four recurring signals, each reported in more detail per model in section 5:

1. **Age is negative.** It replicates for taught receptive vocabulary and word reading, clears z ≥ 2 with weaker stability for expressive vocabulary and nonword repetition, and its SHAP sign is negative in 18 of the 22 gain models (positive only for receptive vocabulary, spelling, word repetition and average articulation, all with negligible importance). At the same starting score, older children in this 5-to-10-year-old cohort gained less over a period. The Bayesian gain-factor model for word reading resolves the same negative age coefficient after the own baseline (`notes/202608201400`).
2. **General language level predicts gains in other language measures.** Expressive information (APT) predicts gains in not-taught expressive words and expressive vocabulary; expressive vocabulary predicts gains in taught expressive words and blending; receptive grammar predicts gains in expressive grammar and expressive vocabulary; basic concepts predict receptive-grammar gain; receptive vocabulary predicts taught-receptive gain. All positive: children with more language elsewhere gained more on the measure in question, over and above the pull of their own baseline.
3. **Speech-sound accuracy and verbal short-term memory predict gains on expressive measures.** DEAP vowel accuracy predicts gains in taught expressive vocabulary and expressive vocabulary; ERB word repetition predicts expressive-grammar gain (and is borderline for expressive information and taught expressive vocabulary); DEAP final-consonant accuracy predicts word-repetition gain. Every one of these outcomes requires the child to produce speech that a tester can score, so a production-side constraint on _measured_ expressive gain is at least as plausible as a developmental mechanism.
4. **A reading-readiness chain.** Letter-sound knowledge predicts word-reading gain; word reading predicts spelling gain and nonword-reading gain; nonword repetition predicts nonword-reading gain (borderline: letter sounds for nonword and not-taught expressive gains). Together with the inverted-U spelling baseline and the floor pattern in nonword reading, this is the same picture the floor-sitter cut drew in July (`notes/202607091318`, section 5): gains on the phonics measures are available to children who already hold the prerequisite skill.

**Design and background variables carry almost nothing in the gain models.** Gender, hearing status, ear-infection history, behaviour rating, number of children in the family, parental post-16 education and home reading never replicate in any of the 22 gain models; the study area clears z ≥ 2 twice (expressive-information and total-repetition gains), unstably both times; period (`time`) is in the top five of half the bootstraps for four models but never resolves (z below 1 in each); and the intervention arm registers only in letter-sound gain (z 1.9, top-5 in 40 % of bootstraps, immediate arm higher). That last signal is consistent with the ITT layer's strongest randomised contrast being letter sounds, but the tree pools all three periods, two of which have both arms treated, so it is not a treatment estimate and should not be quoted as one.

Every replicated level association is positive. The cluster tables show the same three blocks in nearly every model, and the table above is those blocks predicting one another:

- **A language block** {expressive and receptive vocabulary, the Block-1 totals, expressive information, expressive grammar, basic concepts}, with receptive grammar usually clustering just outside it. Any member predicts any other; expressive information (APT) is the most frequent lead. These are concurrent same-wave correlations among tests of overlapping constructs, so the block says more about what the instruments share than about development.
- **A speech-production and verbal-memory block** {DEAP initial and final consonants, ERB word and nonword repetition}. Articulation accuracy predicts repetition scores and repetition predicts articulation, in both directions and with large importances. The ERB is a repetition task, so speech output is part of what it measures; the block is best read as shared production demand, not as phonological memory driving articulation or the reverse. The five language-sample measures attach to this block (initial-consonant accuracy leads four of the five) rather than to the language block.
- **A reading block** {word reading, phonetic spelling, YARC single-word reading, nonword reading}, with letter-sound knowledge clustering separately. Word reading, spelling and nonword reading predict one another; letter sounds predict word reading and spelling.

Two predictors bridge the blocks. **Letter-sound knowledge** replicates for both taught-vocabulary levels (receptive and expressive) as well as for word reading and spelling, and **expressive vocabulary** replicates for word reading as well as across the language block; the Block-1 expressive total replicates for letter-sound level. In these data, a child's phonics and a child's vocabulary are not separate worlds, but the level models cannot say which came first.

Age is positive wherever it registers in the level models (replicated for spelling; z ≥ 2 but unstable for receptive vocabulary, expressive vocabulary and expressive information; a positive lean for initial-consonant articulation and intelligibility), as it should be for raw scores in a cohort spanning five years of age. Background variables are as absent from the level rankings as from the gain rankings, with three isolated and unstable exceptions: hearing status negative for spelling level, number of children negative for receptive vocabulary and final-consonant articulation, and mother's post-16 education negative for word-reading level. None of these should be carried anywhere.

### 3.4 Agreement with the Bayesian layer

The rebuild already wrote the cross-layer comparisons, and they agree with the reading above (`output/statistical_models/comparison/gb_vs_statistical.csv`; `horseshoe_vs_gb.csv` under the four `lrp-rli-hs-*-reporting` directories):

- **Word reading level** (`lrp-rli-gbl-012`): the pooled-levels between-child coefficients put letter sounds first, as does gradient boosting (Spearman +0.70 over five constructs); the regularised-horseshoe ranking (`hs-002`) puts letter sounds first and expressive vocabulary second, where gradient boosting has them second and third behind phonetic spelling, a construct the horseshoe excludes by design.
- **Word reading gain** (`lrp-rli-gbg-012`): the horseshoe (`hs-001`) and gradient boosting agree on the top two, age then letter sounds; the gain-factor model puts age second and letter sounds third after the own baseline that gradient boosting cannot use, so the construct-level rank correlation is −0.11 over seven constructs and the agreement is on the top of the list only.
- **Letter-sound level** (`lrp-rli-gbl-009`) and the horseshoe `hs-004` agree that word reading dominates (both rank it first) with blending third in both.
- **Letter-sound gain** (`lrp-rli-gbg-009`) is the weakest pairing: the horseshoe `hs-003` leads with receptive grammar and basic concepts, gradient boosting with the own baseline, final-consonant articulation and arm, and the top-3 overlap is one construct. Neither method finds much, which is itself the shared conclusion.

The reconciliation note's one-line verdict still holds: **the two layers agree about levels and disagree about change**, because change carries little signal for either to rank.

## 4. Themes, and what they may suggest about predictors of language and reading development

These are interpretive readings of an exploratory layer at n ≈ 54 children. Each would need the Bayesian layer, or a new study, to become a claim.

1. **Where a child starts is the best predictor of where they will be, and the worst predictor of how far they will move.** Concurrent levels are predicted with median R² 0.61 from other concurrent levels; gains are predicted with median R² 0.13, most of it the own baseline pulling scores back toward the mean. Any description of "who progresses" that begins from a child's starting score is mostly describing regression to the mean and the edges of the scales.
2. **Progress is only weakly predictable from measured child characteristics, and not at all from family background.** No gain model explains more than 27 % of held-out variance, eight of 22 replicate nothing beyond the own baseline, and gender, hearing, ear infections, behaviour, family size, parental education and home reading never replicate as predictors of any gain. Read with the Bayesian layer's own findings (at the reporting tier the regularised horseshoe `lrp-rli-hs-001` gives no predictor of word-reading gain a posterior probability above 0.6 of a non-negligible coefficient), this is the negative half of the study's answer about predictors of progress, and it argues against screening children into or out of provision on background characteristics.
3. **Younger children gained more, at a given starting score.** Age is the most consistent non-baseline gain predictor in the suite and its sign is negative in 18 of 22 models. In a cohort aged 5 to 10 that could mean a steeper developmental slope at younger ages, an earlier-is-better effect for this kind of instruction, or an artefact of who was still available to gain on a bounded scale; the gradient-boosting layer cannot separate these. Spelling is the exception (older children gained more), consistent with spelling being the latest-emerging skill in the battery.
4. **Reading skills develop as a chain with a threshold at the floor.** Letter sounds predict word-reading gain; word reading predicts spelling and nonword-reading gain; nonword repetition predicts nonword-reading gain; and the floored measures show that children gain once they hold the prerequisite and hardly before (the inverted-U spelling baseline; two-thirds of children at zero on nonword reading at wave 1). This is the same ordering the mechanism and mediation families model and the survival family follows through time, so the exploratory layer corroborates the DAG's phonics path rather than suggesting a different one.
5. **Language, speech and verbal memory are not separable at the level of these measures, and speech accuracy predicts measured expressive progress.** The level models return one language block and one speech-production block that predict each other in both directions. In the gain models, vowel accuracy, final-consonant accuracy and word repetition predict gains on expressive vocabulary and grammar. The most economical reading is that expressive tests can only credit what a child can articulate, so speech-sound accuracy sets a ceiling on measurable expressive gain. If that is right it is a measurement fact with a practical consequence: expressive-language progress in children with less intelligible speech will be underestimated by these instruments, and the DAG's speech-production node belongs on the measurement side of expressive outcomes, not only among the confounders. The registered gain-factor fits already carry the DEAP composite and the ERB total as adjusters, and their published association marginals give a mixed answer: for expressive vocabulary (`lrp-rli-gf-003`) a one-SD higher DEAP composite goes with 1.5 more words gained (89 % interval −0.6 to +3.6, P(>0) = 0.88) while the ERB total leans the other way (−0.6 words, −2.6 to +1.5, P(>0) = 0.31); for taught expressive vocabulary (`lrp-rli-gf-010`) the DEAP composite is flat (−0.1 words, −0.8 to +0.6, P(>0) = 0.38) and the ERB total is positive (+0.7 words, −0.0 to +1.5, P(>0) = 0.94). So the Bayesian layer supports a speech-accuracy association for expressive vocabulary only, and not decisively; the tree ensemble's signal came from the vowel sub-score, which no registered fit carries.
6. **A general language level predicts language gains.** Children with larger vocabularies, more expressive information or better receptive grammar gained more on other language measures over a period, after their own baseline. This is compatible with a general ability factor, with a rich-get-richer dynamic in vocabulary, and with shared method variance across tests administered on the same day; the tree ensemble cannot tell them apart, and the joint and correlated-factor families are the place that question is actually asked.
7. **Letter sounds and expressive vocabulary bridge reading and language.** Letter-sound knowledge predicts taught-vocabulary levels as well as reading levels, and expressive vocabulary predicts word-reading level and gain. This matches the Bayesian layer's flagship finding that letter sounds are the most robust concurrent correlate of word reading, and it is consistent with an intervention that teaches vocabulary and reading together, but the level models cannot order the two.
8. **The intervention arm surfaces only where the randomised contrast is strongest.** The arm indicator matters in exactly one gain model, letter sounds, with the immediate arm higher. The gradient-boosting layer was not built to estimate treatment effects and pools periods in which both arms are treated, so this is a consistency check on the ITT layer, not evidence for it.

Two limits apply to everything above. The predictors and outcomes are measured at the same wave in the level models and at the start of a period in the gain models, so a stable trait shared across measures, or plain measurement noise, can manufacture any of these associations. And the tuned models are small (a median of 120 trees for the gain models, and 3 to 30 trees in seven cases), which is the honest response to a small sample but means the rankings are coarse below the lead predictor.

**`lrp-rli-gbg-012` — word reading gain (`ewrswr_gain`).** 157 (53); R² 0.08; MAE 3.0 / SD 4.3 (mean 3.5, range −4 to +21; 39 % at zero at wave 1). ## 6. Issues noticed during the review

1. **A site effect on language-sample intelligibility.** `lrp-rli-gbl-026` ranks the study area second, with a clean two-level split of about eight percentage points after articulation is accounted for. Percent-intelligible is a transcriber judgement, so a rater or procedure difference between sites is the first explanation to rule out before the variable enters any statistical model. The language-sample battery is not used by any Bayesian family today (`notes/202607091318`, section 3), so nothing published depends on it.
2. **The two composite gain models are component analyses.** `lrp-rli-gbg-019` (ERB total) and `-022` (DEAP average) include their components as predictors and are led by them; `SAME_SKILL_SIBLINGS` is keyed on level targets only, so no flag is raised. Either drop the components from those two gain models or read them as companions to the component models, which is how section 5 treats them. This is the gain-side counterpart of open residual 3 in the rebuild note (the gain reports cite a same-skill-excluded ranking view that is never produced for gains).
3. **Very small tuned ensembles.** Seven models stop at 30 trees or fewer (three for nonword-repetition gain). That is a legitimate tuning outcome on this sample, but the per-model report reads the same for a 3-tree model as for a 1000-tree one; a line in the report stating the ensemble size would tell a reader how coarse the ranking is.
4. **The word-reading gain model ignores its own baseline** (negative permutation importance). Nothing is wrong with the fit, but a reader of the gain report will see the baseline near the bottom of the ranking and could conclude the baseline does not matter for word-reading gain, when the Bayesian gain-factor model resolves a large own-baseline coupling. The hump-shaped relation is the reason, and the report could say so.
5. **Multiplicity.** Roughly 33 predictors across 50 models produce isolated z ≥ 2 hits for home reading, hearing, family size, behaviour and parental education. None replicates across models, and the cross-model tables in section 3 are the reason this note does not report them as findings.

## 7. Suggested follow-ups

None of these is committed by this note.

- Record the language-sample area effect as a measurement question for the education team (who transcribed at each site, and whether intelligibility was scored to a common rule) before the battery is scoped into the adjusted family.
- Decide the composite-gain question in the same decision as the rebuild note's residual 3, since both are about which same-instrument predictors a gain ranking should carry.
- Add ensemble size (trees, depth) to the per-model report's configuration table; it is already in `config.json`.
- The production-side reading of theme 5 (speech accuracy capping measured expressive gain) is already partly answered by the stored `lrp-rli-gf-003` and `lrp-rli-gf-010` fits, which adjust for the DEAP composite and the ERB total (see theme 5). What no registered fit carries is the DEAP vowel sub-score the gradient-boosting models single out for both expressive-vocabulary gains; a PSIS-LOO comparison of the registered expressive-vocabulary fit against one adjusting for vowel accuracy specifically is the cheapest next step, and it would say whether that association survives the child random intercept and the own-baseline coupling.
