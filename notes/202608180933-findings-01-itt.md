# Findings: the `itt` family — did the intervention work?

**Read `findings-00-overview` first** for the study designs, the two datasets, and how to read credible intervals and evidence labels. This note covers the 28 models in the `itt` family, which carry the project's headline trial results.

## The data

**RLI trial only.** All 28 models use the randomised **timepoint 1 to timepoint 2 window** — the first period, before the waiting-list group started the intervention. This is the single window in the whole study where one randomised group has been treated and the other has not.

Each model uses **one row per child**: the score at timepoint 2, with the child's own timepoint-1 score on the same measure as a covariate. Nothing is stacked, pooled or collapsed across periods. Later timepoints are not used at all — deliberately, because after timepoint 2 both groups have been treated and the randomised comparison is gone.

Most models fit **54 children**. Some fit fewer, and the reason is always a missing outcome rather than an exclusion rule: word reading has 53, phonetic spelling 41, nonword reading 36. The SES-adjusted comparisons drop to 33–34 because socio-economic data are missing for about a third of the sample.

## What the model is for

The question is deliberately narrow: **for children assigned to the intervention rather than the waiting list, how different was the timepoint-2 score?**

The design is an ANCOVA — the outcome regressed on the assigned group, adjusting for the child's own baseline score on that measure and for age. Adjusting for baseline is not there to "control for confounding": randomisation already handles that on average. It is there for **precision** — children who started higher tend to end higher, so removing that variation makes the group comparison sharper. Age enters for the same reason.

Critically, the model adjusts for **the outcome's own baseline and nothing else**. It does not condition on the child's other skills. That is a considered choice: adding more covariates to a randomised comparison can _introduce_ bias if those covariates are themselves affected by the intervention or sit on the causal path, and the additional precision is rarely worth the risk.

Scores are modelled as bounded counts with a Beta-Binomial likelihood, and results are reported in **items** — the number of test items, which is what the number actually means.

## How to read the results

Each row gives the estimated difference between arms in items, with the 89% credible range, and the posterior probability the effect is positive with its evidence label. **Positive means the intervention arm did better.**

Two cautions before the table. First, this is an **available-case modified intention-to-treat estimate**: it starts from children in the archive with an observed outcome, so it is the effect _for the analysed children_, not automatically for all 57 randomised. Second, direction and size are separate claims — several outcomes have strong evidence of a positive direction alongside real uncertainty about whether the size matters practically.

## What was found

The 13 primary outcomes, in descending order of evidence:

| Taught expressive vocabulary (TE)     | **+1.5**       | +0.4 to +2.7 | 0.985 | strong       |
| Word reading (W)                      | **+2.4**       | +0.7 to +4.1 | 0.986 | strong       |

| Taught receptive vocabulary (TR)      | **+1.4**       | +0.2 to +2.5 | 0.968 | moderate     |
| Not-taught receptive vocabulary (UR)  | +0.6           | −0.0 to +1.2 | 0.937 | moderate     |
| Nonword reading (N), off-floor        | +10 pp         | −4 to +24 pp | 0.877 | suggestive   |
| Basic concept knowledge (F)           | +0.9           | −0.3 to +2.0 | 0.891 | suggestive   |
| Not-taught expressive vocabulary (UE) | +0.3           | −0.3 to +1.0 | 0.773 | suggestive   |
| Receptive grammar (T)                 | +0.7           | −0.8 to +2.1 | 0.760 | suggestive   |
| Phonetic spelling (P), off-floor      | +4 pp          | −7 to +16 pp | 0.724 | inconclusive |
| Receptive vocabulary (R)              | +0.2           | −3.7 to +4.3 | 0.539 | inconclusive |
| Expressive vocabulary (E)             | +0.2           | −3.1 to +3.5 | 0.534 | inconclusive |

"pp" is percentage points: for the two floored measures the reported quantity is the change in a child's chance of scoring above zero at all, not a change in score.

**The pattern is coherent and is what a reading intervention should look like if it works.** The largest and best-supported effects are on exactly what the intervention taught directly: letter-sound knowledge, the specific vocabulary items taught, phoneme blending, and word reading. The effects shrink as you move outward to material that was not taught, and disappear entirely on broad standardised vocabulary tests.

That gradient matters. Broad receptive and expressive vocabulary (R and E) are **inconclusive and probably negligible** — the estimate is +0.2 items against a pre-agreed practical threshold of 2 items, and the probability of reaching that threshold is 24% and 19% respectively. This is not a failure to detect something that is there; it is a positive finding about scope. A 20-week intervention shifted the skills it taught and did not shift a general vocabulary measure, which is what you would expect and is worth stating rather than burying. One qualification for anyone reading only this note: the `gain_factors` and `level_factors` families both put a weak **negative** lean on these two measures (suggestive, P(negative) = 0.78 and 0.80–0.91), where the estimate here is flat. The ITT estimate is the best-identified of the three for the randomised question, but the disagreement is real and is set out in those notes.

Note the contrast between **taught** vocabulary (TR +1.4, TE +1.5, both well supported) and **not-taught** vocabulary from the same blocks (UR +0.6, UE +0.3, both weaker). Generalisation beyond the taught items is, at best, suggestive.

## Robustness

The suite includes deliberate replications under different adjustments, and they agree:

- **Ability adjustment.** Refitting with a general-ability covariate leaves the picture unchanged — letter-sound +3.5 (identical), word reading +2.2 versus +2.4, taught expressive +1.5 (identical). The effects are not an artefact of ability imbalance between arms. This covers 8 of the 13 primary outcomes: the vocabulary family (TR, TE, UR, UE, R, E) and the two reading anchors (W, L). Blending, phonetic spelling, nonword reading, basic concepts and grammar have no ability-adjusted counterpart, so this robustness check does not extend to them.
- **Socio-economic adjustment.** On the reduced sample with SES data, word reading is +2.5 adjusted versus +2.4 unadjusted; letter-sound +3.4 adjusted versus +3.9 unadjusted. Matched comparators on the same children rule out the difference being a sample effect.
- **Site adjustment.** Word reading +2.6, letter-sound +3.5 — unchanged.

## The word-reading caveat you should not skip

Word reading is the flagship result and carries the most thorough missing-data analysis, because 4 of the 57 randomised children have no timepoint-2 word-reading score. That analysis materially qualifies the headline:

| Analysis                                                 | Estimate (items) | 89% range    |
| -------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------- | ------------ |
| Headline (53 observed outcomes)                          | +2.4             | +0.7 to +4.1 |
| Same 53, using screening word reading and age            | +2.0             | 0.0 to +4.0  |
| All 57 standardised under a missing-at-random assumption | +2.0             | 0.0 to +4.0  |
| Completing both arms, zero-delta anchor                  | +1.7             | −0.3 to +3.7 |
| **Model-free extreme case**                              | **−6.3 to +4.9** | —            |

Under reasonable assumptions about why data are missing, the effect stays positive and around +2 items. But the final row is the honest bound: if you allow the four missing children to have had _any_ possible outcomes, the direction can reverse. The result is not assumption-free, and the headline should never be quoted without this envelope.

## What these models cannot tell you

**Only the group contrast is causal.** The coefficients on baseline score, age and ability are precision terms. They describe which children scored higher, not what would happen if you changed them. Reading them as effects is the Table 2 fallacy described in the overview.

**These are estimates for the analysed children.** Extending them to all 57 randomised children needs the missing-data assumptions above.

**Nothing here explains _why_ it worked.** The `mediation` and `mechanism` families take up that question, and their answers are associations, not levers.

**Two measures could barely be analysed.** Phonetic spelling and nonword reading are so heavily floored that only 41 and 36 children contribute, and the question had to be weakened to "did the child move off zero". Their inconclusive results reflect thin measurement, not evidence of absence.

Primary outcomes: `itt-001` (TR), `002` (TE), `003` (UR), `004` (UE), `005` (R), `006` (E), `007` (L), `008` (B), `009` (P), `010` (W), `011` (N), `025` (F), `026` (T). Ability-adjusted: `017`–`024`. SES: `013`/`113` adjusted, `014`/`114` matched comparators. Site-adjusted: `027` (W), `028` (L). Guessing-floor companion: `108` (B).

Phoneme blending is a three-choice task, so a child guessing scores about a third correct. `itt-008` uses the ordinary model (+1.0 items) and `itt-108` accounts for guessing explicitly (+0.5 items, 89% −0.1 to +1.1, suggestive). **Neither may be released without the other** — the project requires the paired bundle, because the ordinary model attributes some guessing to skill. The honest summary is that blending improved, with the size sensitive to how guessing is handled.
