# Findings: the `itt` family — did the intervention work?

**Read `findings-00-overview` first** for the study designs, the two datasets, and how to read credible intervals and evidence labels. This note covers the 31 models in the `itt` family, which carry the project's headline trial results.

## The data

**RLI trial only.** All 31 models use the randomised **timepoint 1 to timepoint 2 window** — the first period, before the waiting-list group started the intervention. This is the single window in the whole study where one randomised group has been treated and the other has not.

Each model uses **one row per child** from timepoint 2. The ordinary score models include the child's own timepoint-1 score on the same measure as a precision covariate. The phonetic-spelling and nonword-reading headline models are different: they restrict the analysis to children observed at zero on that measure at timepoint 1, model whether the timepoint-2 score is above zero, and do not include the graded baseline score. Nothing is stacked across periods, and later timepoints are not used.

Most ordinary primary models fit **54 children**; word reading fits 53 because one archived child lacks the required outcome. Phonetic spelling fits 41 and nonword reading 36 because their post-hoc headline estimands include only children observed at the floor at baseline and with the required post-score; nonword reading also has three missing baseline scores. The SES-adjusted comparisons drop to 33–34 because socio-economic data are missing for about a third of the sample.

## What the model is for

The question is deliberately narrow: **for children assigned to the intervention rather than the waiting list, how different was the timepoint-2 score — or, for the two exploratory floor-rule models, the chance of moving above zero?**

For the ordinary score outcomes, the design is an ANCOVA — the outcome regressed on assigned group, adjusting for the child's own baseline score on that measure and for age. Adjusting for baseline is not there to "control for confounding": randomisation already handles that on average. It is there for **precision** — children who started higher tend to end higher, so removing that variation makes the group comparison sharper. Age enters for the same reason. The two off-floor headline models instead adjust for age within their baseline-floor subgroups.

Critically, each unadjusted ordinary primary model uses **the outcome's own baseline and no other skill measure**. That is a considered choice: adding post-treatment skills could condition on the causal path. The registered ability-, SES- and site-adjusted robustness models add only their declared pre-treatment covariates. The off-floor models use baseline floor status to define eligibility rather than fitting a graded own-baseline term.

Ordinary scores are modelled as bounded counts with a Beta-Binomial likelihood and reported in **items**. Phonetic spelling and nonword reading use a Bernoulli likelihood for moving above zero and are reported as risk differences in percentage points.

## How to read the results

Each row gives the estimated difference between arms in items for ordinary scores or percentage points for the two off-floor outcomes, with the 89% credible range and the posterior probability the effect is positive with its evidence label. **Positive means the intervention arm did better.**

Two cautions before the table. First, these are **available-case modified intention-to-treat estimates**: they start from children in the archive with the required observations, so they do not automatically estimate the effect for all 57 randomised children. The two off-floor results are narrower still: exploratory post-hoc subgroup contrasts among children observed at zero at baseline. Second, direction and size are separate claims — several outcomes have strong evidence of a positive direction alongside real uncertainty about whether the size matters practically.

"pp" is percentage points: for the two floored measures the reported quantity is the arm difference in the chance of scoring above zero at timepoint 2 **among children observed at zero at baseline**, not a change in score for the full analysed cohort.

**The pattern is compatible with effects concentrated on directly taught skills.** The largest and best-supported estimates are for letter-sound knowledge, the specific vocabulary items taught, phoneme blending, and word reading. Estimates are smaller for material that was not taught, while the broad standardised vocabulary estimates sit near zero with wide intervals.

That gradient matters, but it does not prove absence. Broad receptive and expressive vocabulary (R and E) are **inconclusive**: the median is about +0.2 items, but the 89% ranges extend roughly 3–4 items in either direction. Against the project's post-hoc 2-item practical threshold, the probabilities of a benefit that large are 24% and 19%, while 58% and 67% of the posterior respectively lie inside the ±2-item region of practical equivalence. Under these models a benefit of at least 2 items is therefore unlikely, not ruled out. The `gain_factors` and `level_factors` specifications instead lean weakly negative on these measures; their intervals overlap the ITT intervals, so this is specification sensitivity rather than a demonstrated contradiction.

Note the contrast between **taught** vocabulary (TR +1.4, TE +1.5, both well supported) and **not-taught** vocabulary from the same blocks (UR +0.6, UE +0.3, both smaller). UR has moderate directional evidence and UE suggestive directional evidence under the fixed ladder, but their magnitudes are modest and uncertain. The results therefore permit some generalisation beyond the taught items without establishing how much; the dependence-limited taught-minus-not-taught contrasts are discussed in the `joint` note.

## The Action Picture Test outcomes, added after the main run

Two outcomes in the table above were fitted later than the rest and deserve their own note.
The Action Picture Test (Renfrew 1997) measures expressive language, and the project's causal
diagram has always treated both its scales as direct outcomes of randomised assignment. Neither
had ever been estimated, because the test's ceilings were unconfirmed — the same class of
blocker that still withholds six historical models. They were confirmed from the manual on
2026-08-18 (Grammar 37, Information 40) and both scales were then fitted.

**Expressive grammar (EG)** is the more informative of the two: **+1.0 marks of 37** (89% −0.6 to
+2.5), with a 0.849 probability of being positive — suggestive, and the strongest result among
the outcomes that were not directly taught. The difference-in-differences fit agrees and is a
little stronger, at +1.6 marks (89% −0.2 to +3.4) with a probability of 0.926, which is moderate.
Neither clears zero, so this is a lean rather than a finding, but it is a consistent lean on a
measure the intervention did not teach directly.

**Expressive information (EI)** is inconclusive: **+0.2 marks of 40** (89% −1.5 to +1.9),
probability 0.563, with 65% of the posterior inside the practically-negligible region. Its
difference-in-differences fit is also inconclusive (probability 0.741). One caveat belongs with
it: the arm-by-wave model puts the pre-treatment arm gap at 0.757 probability positive, so the
immediate arm may have started slightly ahead on this measure — a balance quantity worth noting
where it is not for the other outcomes.

**A scale decision sits behind EI.** Information awards half marks on some items, so 44% of its
raw values are not whole numbers and the bounded-count likelihood rejects them outright. The
modelled outcome is therefore the doubled half-mark scale, out of 80. Doubling is exact — every
fractional part is 0.5 — and leaves the proportion untouched, but it asserts 80 trials where
there are 40 partly-credited items, which in principle overstates how much each child tells us.
A companion model (`itt-129`) rounds to whole marks and fits out of 40 to check whether that
matters. It does not: on the model's internal scale the two give +0.019 and +0.012 with posterior
standard deviations of 0.1234 and 0.1231 — indistinguishable. The reason is visible in the fits:
the overdispersion parameter falls from 46 to 28 between them, almost exactly cancelling the
doubled trial count. The encoding choice is safe here, and it was tested rather than assumed.

One further note on thresholds. Every outcome in this suite is judged against a practical threshold δ, and the two APT thresholds were derived by the project's existing rule (half the untaught arm's natural gain, floored at one mark) rather than agreed in advance: grammar 1 of 37, information 1 of 40. Information's value sits just under a rounding boundary — 1.49 halved — so 2 marks would have been equally rule-consistent and would have lowered its reported chance of a practically-meaningful benefit; it was therefore escalated rather than settled by the analysis. **Both were ratified on 2026-08-18** (`notes/202608182015-apt-delta-threshold-ratification.md`), so the magnitude statements here stand on the same footing as the rest of the suite. They remain post-hoc thresholds, as all of this suite's are.

## Robustness

The suite includes deliberate sensitivity checks under different adjustments, and their estimates are similar:

- **Ability adjustment.** Refitting with a general-ability covariate leaves the picture similar — letter-sound +3.5 (identical), word reading +2.2 versus +2.4, taught expressive +1.5 (identical). This gives little indication that measured ability imbalance drives those estimates. It covers 8 of the 15 primary outcomes: the vocabulary family (TR, TE, UR, UE, R, E) and the two reading anchors (W, L). Blending, phonetic spelling, nonword reading, basic concepts and grammar have no ability-adjusted counterpart, so this robustness check does not extend to them.
- **Socio-economic adjustment.** On the reduced sample with SES data, word reading is +2.5 adjusted versus +2.4 unadjusted; letter-sound +3.4 adjusted versus +3.9 unadjusted. Matched comparators on the same children rule out the difference being a sample effect.
- **Site adjustment.** Word reading +2.6, letter-sound +3.5 — unchanged.

## The word-reading caveat you should not skip

Word reading is the flagship result and carries the most thorough missing-data analysis, because 4 of the 57 randomised children have no timepoint-2 word-reading score. That analysis materially qualifies the headline:

| Analysis                                                 | Estimate (items) | 89% range    |
| -------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------- | ------------ |
| Headline (53 observed outcomes)                          | +2.4             | +0.7 to +4.1 |
| Same 53, using screening word reading and age            | +2.0             | 0.0 to +4.0  |
| All 57 standardised under a missing-at-random assumption | +2.0             | 0.0 to +4.0  |
| Completing both arms, zero-delta anchor                  | +1.7             | −0.3 to +3.7 |
| **Model-free extreme case**                              | **−6.3 to +4.9** | —            |

Under reasonable assumptions about why data are missing, the effect stays positive and around +2 items. But the final row is the honest bound: if you allow the four missing children to have had _any_ possible outcomes, the direction can reverse. The result is not assumption-free, and the headline should never be quoted without this envelope.

## What these models cannot tell you

**Only the assigned-group contrast is causal, subject to the analysis-set assumptions above.** Where fitted, the coefficients on baseline score, age and ability are precision terms. They describe which children scored higher, not what would happen if you changed them. Reading them as effects is the Table 2 fallacy described in the overview.

**These are estimates for the analysed children.** Extending them to all 57 randomised children needs the missing-data assumptions above.

**Nothing here explains _why_ it worked.** The `mediation` and `mechanism` families take up that question, and their answers are associations, not levers.

**Two measures required exploratory subgroup analyses.** For phonetic spelling and nonword reading, the post-hoc floor rule restricts the headline to 41 and 36 children respectively who were observed at zero at baseline and had the required post-score. The question is only whether the chance of moving above zero differs between arms in that subgroup. The broad uncertainty is not evidence of absence, and the result does not apply to children already above the floor.

## Model inventory

All 28 models pass the convergence gate with zero divergences and are publishable. Primary outcomes: `itt-001` (TR), `002` (TE), `003` (UR), `004` (UE), `005` (R), `006` (E), `007` (L), `008` (B), `009` (P), `010` (W), `011` (N), `025` (F), `026` (T). Ability-adjusted: `017`–`024`. SES: `013`/`113` adjusted, `014`/`114` matched comparators. Site-adjusted: `027` (W), `028` (L). Guessing-floor companion: `108` (B).

Phoneme blending is a three-choice task, so a child guessing scores about a third correct. `itt-008` uses the ordinary model (+1.0 items) and `itt-108` accounts for guessing explicitly (+0.5 items, 89% −0.1 to +1.1, suggestive). **Neither may be released without the other** — the project requires the paired bundle, because the ordinary model attributes some guessing to skill. The honest summary is that blending improved, with the size sensitive to how guessing is handled.
